# Facilitator Guide

## 1. Facilitator stance

The facilitator is not there to sell a model, prove AI is wonderful, or prove AI is terrible. The job is to help participants make better decisions.

Use the AIWF teaching pattern:

1. principle first
2. concrete example second
3. guided practice third
4. documented decision last

Keep the room beginner-safe. Never reward jargon for its own sake. A participant who can correctly decide “do not upload this file” has demonstrated more useful competence than someone who can name six transformer architectures while casually feeding payroll into a public chatbot.

## 2. Delivery rules

- define every technical term the first time it appears
- use public or fictional examples only
- keep demonstrations short
- show failures, not only polished outputs
- separate creativity tasks from factual tasks
- ask participants to explain their review decision
- never imply that a generated citation is real until it has been opened and checked
- never use the facilitator's authority as a substitute for current source verification
- mark current vendor or product behavior with the date checked

## 3. Accessibility

Provide:

- large-print and digital materials
- captions for video or live demonstrations
- keyboard-accessible documents and forms
- plain-language summaries
- high-contrast slides
- verbal descriptions of diagrams
- breaks at predictable times
- options to participate without creating a commercial AI account

Avoid exercises that assume fast typing, perfect vision, advanced reading level, or comfort speaking in front of the group.

## 4. Room and equipment setup

Minimum:

- display or projector
- facilitator laptop
- stable internet or a prepared offline fallback
- printed workbooks
- timer
- sticky notes or index cards
- sanitized source packets

Recommended:

- one support person for every 12-15 learners
- power strips
- loaner devices
- demo accounts
- a local or privacy-preserving model for comparison where practical

## 5. Demonstration environment

Before each session:

- sign out of personal accounts
- clear prior chat history or use a fresh workspace
- disable browser autofill where confidential information could appear
- verify screen-sharing boundaries
- prepare sanitized prompts in a text file
- test the tool and network
- keep screenshots for the offline fallback

Tiny robot note: check the account before projecting the universe.

## 6. Handling sensitive information

If a participant begins to paste sensitive information:

1. stop the action immediately
2. do not ask them to read the information aloud
3. close or clear the input where possible
4. notify the designated host contact
5. document what type of information was exposed, not unnecessary copies of the information itself
6. follow the organization's incident procedure
7. do not continue the same exercise until the risk is understood

The facilitator should never promise that deletion guarantees removal from every vendor system.

## 7. Teaching prompting

Do not teach prompting as secret incantations. Teach it as task specification.

A useful prompt makes these things reviewable:

- task
- context
- audience
- constraints
- allowed source material
- output format
- required verification

Use side-by-side examples. Ask participants to identify which missing instruction caused the failure.

## 8. Teaching verification

Demonstrate at least one convincing false answer. Then show the verification path.

Required checks should include:

- names
- dates
- numbers
- quotations
- links
- citations
- high-impact recommendations

For numerical claims, recompute from the source. For quotations, search the original. For links, open them. A citation-shaped decoration is not evidence.

## 9. Teaching governance without freezing the room

Governance should answer practical questions:

- what may we do?
- what may we not do?
- what information may be used?
- who approves tools?
- who reviews output?
- what happens when something goes wrong?

Avoid turning the policy sprint into a six-month constitutional convention. The pilot goal is an interim one-page policy that leadership can review.

## 10. Common participant reactions

### “We are too small to need a policy.”

Response: small organizations often rely on personal accounts and informal habits, which makes simple rules more valuable, not less.

### “Nobody here uses AI.”

Response: ask about built-in writing tools, phone photo tools, search summaries, meeting assistants, design tools, office suites, and volunteers.

### “We cannot use AI because it makes mistakes.”

Response: identify which low-risk tasks can tolerate a draft-and-review process. The answer may still be “not yet” for high-impact work.

### “The tool says our data is private.”

Response: open the current policy and identify exactly what private means, for which plan, under which settings, and as of what date.

### “Can AI make this decision for us?”

Response: separate assistance from delegation. High-impact decisions require accountable human ownership and, often, professional review.

## 11. Evaluation rubric

Score final organizational work from 0-2 in each category:

| Category | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data classification | Missing | Partial or unclear | Clear green/yellow/red rules |
| Tool approval | No owner | Informal owner | Named role and review process |
| Human review | Not defined | General statement | Specific checks and approver |
| Prohibited uses | Missing | Narrow list | Covers sensitive data and high-impact decisions |
| Workflow documentation | Missing | Incomplete | Inputs, tool, review, output, fallback documented |
| Incident response | Missing | Contact only | Reporting, containment, owner, follow-up defined |

A score of 9 or higher out of 12 indicates pilot readiness for the documented low-risk workflow.

## 12. Session closeout

At the end of every session, collect:

- one thing the participant can now explain
- one decision their organization still needs to make
- one part of the material that was confusing
- one risk or concern that surfaced

Use this feedback to revise the next session. The curriculum is a living system, not a ceremonial tablet.
